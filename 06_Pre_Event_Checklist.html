Gap analysis — things not in the original plan

These are not in the invite, the agenda, or your task list. Most are small; a few will cause a visible problem on the day if they are missed.

1. The prize doesn't exist yet

"Prize Award" is on the published agenda and in the invite, so attendees are expecting something at 12:25. Nothing has been decided or bought.

Decide this week. It does not need to be expensive — Snowflake swag, a team dinner voucher, or a named certificate all work. What matters is that something is physically handed over in front of the room.

2. Dietary requirements were never collected

The registration form captured name, firm, email and role — no dietary field. You are catering for 30 people across ten firms with no allergy or dietary information.

Add one line to the first email asking people to reply with requirements, and brief Cliftons once you have them. This is the kind of thing that is invisible when handled and memorable when not.

3. Photography and social consent

You will want photos of a room full of partners building on Snowflake. Some firms — particularly the Big Four — have strict policies on staff appearing in vendor marketing.

Mention photography in the first email with an opt-out, and say it again verbally at 08:30. Cheap insurance against an awkward conversation with a partner's marketing team afterwards.

4. Accessibility was never asked about

No accessibility question on the registration form, and a hands-on terminal-based lab has real accessibility considerations — screen readers, font sizing, mobility access at Parmelia House.

Add an open "anything we should know to make the day work for you?" line to the first email. It covers accessibility, dietary and laptop issues in one non-intrusive question.

5. Nobody owns the commercial follow-up

Twenty-five people from ten partner firms, including three in leadership roles and two in sales, will leave the room warm. The agenda ends at 12:30 and the plan ends with it.

Decide before the event who owns the follow-up conversation with each of the ten firms, and what you are asking them for — a practice commitment, a named client opportunity, a follow-on session. The badge is the enablement outcome; this is the business one.

6. Badge completion still needs chasing — but you now have a head start

The assessment is self-paced and sits after the event. Attendance is not completion, and you have committed to partners that they can earn the badge.

Pre-enrolment is now Prerequisite 4 in both emails, which removes the enrolment friction and lets you see who is enrolled before the day. The remaining job is completion: put the claim path on screen at 12:25, repeat it in the follow-up email, and chase at two and four weeks with named individuals. Track it on the roster. Telling the room you will report completion back to their firm lifts completion sharply.

7. There is no plan for people who finish the lab early

Nineteen of twenty-five attendees are technical delivery people. A meaningful number will finish Modules 0–5 well inside the hour, and disengaged early finishers are disruptive.

Stretch tasks are built into the lab guide, and the strongest move is to ask early finishers to help their table. Brief the Lab Lead to say this out loud at the 10:34 checkpoint rather than leaving it to chance.

8. A single partner firm could dominate the hackathon

FA-Tech has six registrants and InterWorks five. Left to self-select, each would form its own team — losing the cross-firm networking that is one of the real reasons partners attend.

Pre-assigned, mixed teams with printed name cards, as specified in the run sheet. Announce the mixing as deliberate so it reads as a feature rather than an inconvenience.

9. There is no offline fallback for a total network failure

Everything in the session depends on reaching Snowflake. The venue guest network with 30 concurrent users is a genuine risk and no contingency existed.

The run sheet now carries one: Matthew demos on a hotspot, the hackathon converts to a whiteboard design exercise on the same three tracks, and the lab goes home as homework with the badge link. Have the whiteboard or flipchart in the room so this is actually executable.

10. Six teams in a 25-minute pitch slot has no slack

Six pitches at three minutes plus one minute of questions is 24 of your 25 minutes. One team overrunning by two minutes eats the entire buffer and pushes into the close.

A visible countdown timer and an MC willing to cut people off mid-sentence. Brief the judges to ask exactly one question each. Tell the teams at 11:58 that the three minutes is hard — it is kinder than cutting them off unwarned.

11. The hackathon has no tie-break or scoring mechanics on the day

Three judges scoring independently across four criteria for six teams needs a sheet and an agreed process, or you will be doing mental arithmetic in front of the room.

Pre-printed score sheets, and the judges confer for two minutes while the Event Owner fills time. The tie-breaker is published in the brief: whichever solution the judges would most readily take into a client conversation.

12. Two of the five Snowflake staff are double-booked during pitches

The Lab Lead and Floor 1 are both judging while Floor 2 covers support. If two teams' demos break simultaneously during pitches, there is one person to help.

Acceptable risk, but know it. The mitigation in the run sheet is that a team whose demo fails moves to the end of the pitch order, giving Floor 2 time to work the problem.
